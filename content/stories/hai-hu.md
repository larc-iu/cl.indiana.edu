Title: Linguistics and NLP—why not both?
Date: 2025-12-01
Alum: Hai Hu, Ph.D. 2021
Role: Assistant Professor, The Hong Kong Polytechnic University
Image: images/people/hai_hu.jpg
Quote: “With the linguistic background, for NLP we provide the linguistic perspective, but we are also learning techniques and tools from other disciplines—math, cognitive science, computer science.”
Summary: Hai Hu trained as a generative syntactician and became a computational linguist without leaving linguistics behind, and now runs a research program that draws on both.

Hai Hu came to Indiana to work on generative syntax and stayed to become a computational
linguist. Hu finished the Ph.D. in six years with a minor in cognitive science,
internships at three companies, and published work with collaborators in mathematics,
Chinese, and Spanish. Hu is now an assistant professor in the Department of Language Science
and Technology at The Hong Kong Polytechnic University, where his research—benchmarking
language models on Chinese reasoning, computational modeling for psycholinguistics,
treebanking—draws on both halves of that training.

**How did you come to work across linguistics and computational linguistics?**

My story is a little complicated. I studied English literature in my undergrad, and I felt
literature was really not for me. So I switched to linguistics for my M.A. Then I went to 
Germany for a one-year exchange program, where I was first introduced to computational 
linguistics. The professor there was an IU faculty member's advisor, and he suggested 
several places to apply for a Ph.D.

But when I got to IU, I started out as a generative syntactician. After
one year, I realized my interest was more aligned with computational linguistics, so I
switched. I worked with three CL faculty—Damir Cavar, Sandra Kübler, and Markus
Dickinson—and with Larry Moss from the math department. I would say I really benefited
from the interdisciplinary atmosphere in linguistics and at IU.

**How much room did the program give you to work in both fields?**

I think the most important thing is the openness, and the range of possibilities. If I had
done a Ph.D. somewhere else—for example in China, or in Europe—I think it would have been
very difficult for me to switch from theoretical linguistics to computational linguistics.
When you enter those programs you are already almost set. You just do a dissertation.

I also looked at a lot of other people, people who were English majors or theoretical
linguists who switched to computational linguistics. Most of them did a master's degree in CL
first, to get more background. I was very lucky that I could do this inside a Ph.D. program,
and I finished in six years.

**How does the program bring linguists up to speed on the computational side?**

In general I think the courses in CL are really good courses for people with zero background. 
I took one or two CL courses before I took the computer science
courses—if I had started with the computer science courses, I wouldn't have been able to
follow. So the CL courses are a great introductory-level stepping stone for later.

In my first year, syntax was my main focus, and I didn't write any code. In my second year
computational linguistics became my main focus, and I took three or four computer science
courses that year. Altogether I probably took five or six CS courses, plus about five in
cognitive science, which I took as a minor. I was still taking courses in my fourth or fifth
year, which was not very fun, but I think it was necessary to get the engineering skills.

One instructor in the computer science department was really helpful. She taught data
structures really well. I spent something like twenty hours a week on the homework, but I
really learned a lot from that course, and a lot of it is still useful—especially for
theoretical computational linguistics, where you have to use tree structures and graphs.

**What support made that workload possible?**

I was on a fellowship from the China Scholarship Council. It lasts four years, so I didn't
have to teach, and I could focus on courses and research for the first four years. That
helped a lot—if you have to TA, sometimes twenty hours a week is gone.

I also did a lot of programming in the summers. I worked as an RA for a sociology professor
on information extraction in Chinese. That was the first project I ever did in Python. I
wrote more than a thousand lines of Python and about a hundred regular expressions—I remember
it very clearly, because he had been talking to computer scientists who said it should be
done with machine learning. At that time I didn't know machine learning, so I said, okay,
I'm just going to write regular expressions.

**You published with faculty well outside linguistics. How does that happen?**

Larry gave a talk on monotonicity inference. I thought it was really interesting, so I talked
to him afterward. He had an idea for an algorithm and wanted to implement it in CCG. I think
he asked whether I wanted to do that, and I said yes, and we started collaborating. He would
provide the theory and the algorithm, and I would implement it. That's how he introduced me
to natural language inference, which at the time was a hot topic in NLP.

I also collaborated with Charles Lin in East Asian Languages and Cultures, and with Patrícia
Amaral in Spanish and Portuguese. It's a really good atmosphere where you can have these kinds of
collaborations, and we have publications. I think that's what I benefited from the most—this
very collaborative atmosphere. And it's not just me. Many people collaborate with different
people from different departments.

**Did you do internships?**

I did an internship at Megaputer, which is in Bloomington. I think I also worked one summer
for Nuance and one for ETS. Except for the first one, they were all during COVID, so they
were remote.

It's really helpful to have industry experience, because you know what the industry is really
using—and it turns out that sometimes they're not using really fancy stuff. They wanted
rule-based systems. Machine learning is fancy, but they judged it probably wouldn't work in
real production. I was writing rules for a system in my Mandarin dialect. You think you
should do something fancier, but that's what works in production.

The professors in CL really support this. In other departments, sometimes the professors
don't want their students to do internships, because they want them working on their
projects.

**How did you decide between academia and industry?**

Because of the China Scholarship Council fellowship, I had to go back to China to work for
two years. But I think it's a good deal—they pay for four years of full tuition and stipend,
which is really a lot.

I didn't look for industry jobs. At that time it was COVID, so there weren't many jobs
anyway. I applied for some postdoc programs in the U.S. and in Europe, and I got an
interview, but no offer. Then I applied to dozens of universities in China and got two or
three offers.

Some people in my cohort liked industry jobs. After they got candidacy they kept doing
internships, and it seemed that they enjoyed it. But I don't like being told what to do. I'd
rather say, okay, I want to do this, so I do this. So an academic job seemed better for me.
What nobody tells you is that you have to write a lot of grants. It's not like writing
papers. Writing grants is more important.

**What would you say to someone considering the program?**

At IU there's a lot of collaboration between disciplines, and there's the cognitive science
program, which brings together a lot of different people with different backgrounds. With the
linguistic background, for NLP we provide the linguistic perspective, but we are also
learning techniques and tools from other disciplines, such as math and cognitive science and
computer science. You can do a lot of stuff—as long as you want to switch, and you work hard
at it.
