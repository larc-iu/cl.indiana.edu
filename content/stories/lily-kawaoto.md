Title: Knowing what's under the hood
Date: 2025-12-01
Alum: Lily Kawaoto, M.A. 2023
Role: Leidos
Quote: “Being able to explain how a transformer works, why it spits out the next token the way it does, and being able to relay that understanding, gave me an advantage over my colleagues.”
Summary: Lily Kawaoto came to Indiana from UCLA set on industry rather than academia. Tuition was covered by an associate instructorship, an advisor made the introduction that led to an internship, and a full-time offer arrived a year before graduation.

Lily Kawaoto chose Indiana over two other master's programs for a simple reason: the faculty
answered their email. Two years later, with tuition covered by an associate instructorship
and a full-time offer already in hand, Kawaoto finished the M.A. in Computational
Linguistics and went directly into industry at Leidos, starting as a data scientist and
since moving into a business role—where being able to explain how the technology actually
works has turned out to be the advantage.

**What was your background coming out of undergrad, and why a master's in CL?**

My undergrad was two degrees, one in cognitive science and the other called "linguistics and
computer science," which was UCLA's way of saying computational linguistics. But it was a bit
disjointed—it was just taking computer science classes and taking linguistics classes, maybe
one or two electives that kind of merged the two, but not a proper foundation in actual CL.

Coming out of undergrad it was during COVID, so I was kind of wondering, okay, is it even a
good time to find a job? Does it make sense to do a research path, going into academia?
Eventually I decided it wouldn't hurt to get a master's in CL specifically, because I knew
that I wanted to do something in the tech industry.

**What drew you to Indiana in particular?**

I could talk to the professors. Some of the professors did a panel where prospective
students could talk to them and see what research projects they're working on, and whether it
aligns with their own interests. I was considering two other schools, and the professors there, 
at least the ones I tried to reach out to, weren't very responsive.

That was huge, in my opinion. Being able to talk to a professor you'll be working with in
grad school is important. Even though it wasn't really in person, at least I was able to have
face-to-face conversations with them virtually. That was a deciding factor.

**Once you arrived, was that impression correct?**

Oh yeah. My advisor, Sandra Kübler, was great. I still try to keep in touch with her. All
good things to say.

**How did you find your first job?**

That was actually through Sandra. Earlier that year, or late 2021, I was looking for
internships for the following summer. I didn't have any luck, and eventually I went to Sandra
and just asked, do you know anyone who is looking for an intern? And she was able to connect
me to a manager at Leidos who was looking for one.

What was kind of serendipitous was that this manager was looking for someone who had some
experience, or at least some interest, in speech recognition. I wouldn't say I formally
started, but I was interested in it while I was at IU, and so that interest just happened to
align. That's how I was able to get the internship. And after that internship, I was offered
a full-time job after I graduated. I'm still there.

**What did the work look like?**

Initially I worked on a speech emotion recognition project I started during my internship, 
which I was able to continue for a little bit afterwards, once I came back full-time.

The team I'm on is kind of unique. It's called an accelerator, and it has some R&D components
to it, so some team members do research and NLP tasks, or whatever data science use cases
make sense for the company. But then there are also people who get pulled into other parts of
the company, because they need someone who has knowledge in AI and ML.

So I was kind of doing all of that—multiple use cases in different parts of the company.
Sometimes it was for cybersecurity, sometimes it was for health, sometimes it was for the
marketing and communications team. It was just very varied, though the core data science work
I was doing was mostly the same. At that point ChatGPT was everywhere, so a lot of the use cases
evolved around that—prompt engineering and RAG, and being able to customize the underlying
technology to different audiences.

**How has your role changed since then?**

I started out as a data scientist, so I was still doing NLP things. Now I've evolved more
into a business role. But my technical background has been an advantage, because now I can
talk to the developers on an engineering team in more of the technical jargon—I'm able to
relay the message better—and also just explain to people who don't have a technical
background, like, how does an LLM work? It's transformers on the back end, and then they
would ask follow-up questions, like, why does it do X when I want it to do Y? Having that CL
background has helped me better explain to people not necessarily from technical backgrounds
how things work. And that's been really rewarding.

**What backgrounds did your colleagues have, and what was your comparative advantage?**

Most of them had some kind of data science background, if they had a data science major in
college. Others were more computer engineering, or other kinds of engineering backgrounds,
but still involved coding. They maybe had a little bit more of an advantage, because they had
done their undergrad in that as well as their master's.

Linguistics-wise—well, it's more CL than pure linguistics. But being able to explain how a
transformer works, why it spits out the next token the way it does, and being able to relay
that understanding, gave me an advantage over my colleagues.

**What advice would you give someone considering a master's in CL?**

I would do my degree over again. I'm just a little bit hesitant, because when I left, that
was kind of the beginning of generative AI, and the hype for that has grown exponentially, so
I don't know what the courses look like now, or whether students are allowed to use ChatGPT
or Claude.

But a lot of jobs now are wanting to bring it in, if they haven't already, so I think it's
good practical experience. And especially when it comes to the topics the CL courses at IU
teach, I think it's still applicable to industry use cases.

If students are able to do the master's at IU, do it. The faculty are great, and the faculty
cover every major area of CL.
